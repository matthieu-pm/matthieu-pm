# UX writing course: complete article outline

Research checked on 2026-09-16 against the 123-slide Canva presentation [Introduction to User Experience Writing](https://canva.link/cours-ux-writing-25-26-en). The presentation is the primary source throughout this note. Slide references use the number shown in Canva.

## Recommendation in one sentence

Turn the current short guide into a structured beginner course: explain the discipline, show how it fits into product work, teach the writing rules and component patterns, then extend into research, voice, content systems, accessibility, AI, and a final audit. Keep the existing five interactions, add the five already scaffolded in `ux-writing-lab.tsx`, and add a brand-voice cursor plus an AI prompt lab or final audit so the article has 11–12 purposeful exercises rather than five isolated demos.

## Source cautions

- Several slides are visual examples rather than written explanations. Descriptions below marked **visual reading** are interpretations of what the slide demonstrates, not quotations.
- The ROI figures on slides 67–72 and the language-performance claim on slide 113 are claims reproduced by the course from third-party sources. They were not independently validated for this task. The article should frame them as course examples, not universal facts.
- The EuroMillions results slide says variation 2 was chosen even though the displayed variant numbering and metrics appear ambiguous. Do not reproduce that winner as a factual case-study conclusion without checking the original experiment (slides 70–72).
- The deck sometimes uses `Content Designer` and `UX Writer` for overlapping work. The article should explain the relationship rather than imply that every organization uses the titles identically (slides 17–21, 108–109).
- The deck's AI-tool list is time-sensitive. Teach the jobs AI can help with; do not make a long product directory the durable core of the article (slides 109–110).

## Implementation-ready article structure

### 1. Words are part of the interface

Explain what UX writing is, what microcopy is, where it appears, and how it differs from marketing copy, editorial content, and storytelling. Use Doctolib, Netflix, and Airbnb as a compact “spot the writing” gallery (slides 3–12).

**Keep:** the existing “Find the microcopy” exercise.

**Add:** `IsThisUxWriting` — six cards such as a button, help article, ad headline, error, onboarding step, and social caption. Learners classify each by its primary job. Feedback should explain that context and function matter more than length.

### 2. UX writing, content design, and the product team

Clarify the nested relationship: interface copy is one part of content design, and the role collaborates across discovery, build, and delivery. Retain the lifecycle cards, then add a concise collaboration workflow: research language → prototype copy → test understanding → document the decision → iterate (slides 17–28).

**Article-only section:** this is conceptual and does not need another interaction.

### 3. Clear, concise, useful

Teach the three principles as a sequence, using the course's authentication-error ladder: generic failure → specific problem → shorter label → recovery actions. Explain that “concise” never means deleting context the user needs (slides 30–35).

**Keep:** “Choose good over vague” and “Replace the jargon.”

**Add:** `ClarityLadder` — give learners a generic error and let them add four blocks: specific title, cause/context, recovery action, and alternative route. Score against clear/concise/useful, with an explanation for each dimension.

### 4. Sentence-level craft

Expand the rule list into short before/after examples for jargon, active voice, conversational language, localization, specificity, scan-friendly structure, and consistent terms (slides 36–44). This should be a real teaching section rather than a compact list.

**Add:** `ConsistencyCheck` — show a two-step flow with mismatched labels (`Next`, `Continue`, `Go back`, `Previous`) and ask learners to normalize the terms. A component with this name is already scaffolded in `ux-writing-lab.tsx` but is not rendered by the current article.

### 5. Write for the component

Give each component a repeatable formula (slides 45–52):

- CTA: action + truthful outcome; add benefit only when it reduces uncertainty.
- Loading: what is happening + whether the user should wait or act.
- Onboarding: benefit + one concept per step + visible progress/skip when appropriate.
- Empty state: what belongs here + why it is empty + a first action.
- 404: what happened + route back to useful destinations.
- Error: specific problem + relevant reason + recovery + alternative/exit.

**Keep:** “Make the CTA tell the truth.”

**Add:** `BuildAnErrorMessage` — learners assemble title, explanation, primary recovery, and secondary exit. The component already exists in `ux-writing-lab.tsx` but is not rendered.

### 6. Audit a complete flow

Use the PlayStation before/after and the good/bad gallery to teach a six-pass audit: user goal, clarity, next action, consistency, tone/pressure, and accessibility. Explain that an individual sentence can be acceptable while the sequence is confusing (slides 53–66).

**Add:** `AuditTheFlow` — a three-screen flow with several issues. Ask learners to select issues by category, then reveal a prioritized audit. The component already exists in `ux-writing-lab.tsx` but is not rendered.

### 7. Measure copy as a product decision

Use “Book a room” versus “Check availability” to show behavior-accurate labels. Explain the A/B-test chain: hypothesis → variants → primary behavior → guardrail → decision. Mention the ROI and EuroMillions numbers only as examples reported by the course and avoid implying guaranteed uplift (slides 67–72).

**Optional interaction:** `ChooseTheMetric` — for a CTA experiment, choose the success metric and guardrail before seeing results. This teaches that “the version with the most clicks” is not automatically the best product outcome.

### 8. Learn the user's language

Teach lightweight semantic research: collect real words from interviews, support, store reviews, social posts, and product analytics; group repeated goals and friction; distinguish evidence from assumptions; then produce a short behavioral profile (slides 74–78, 88–90).

**Add:** `FindUserLanguage` — select which phrases from a small review set belong in interface copy and which are internal jargon. This component already exists in `ux-writing-lab.tsx` but is not rendered.

### 9. Voice stays; tone moves

Keep the current definition, but add the brand-personality questions, archetypes as inspiration rather than rules, and a voice-chart method. For example: select positions on three sliders, write one invariant voice principle, then adapt it to celebration, uncertainty, and failure (slides 79–86).

**Keep:** “Tune the tone to the moment.”

**Add:** `VoiceCursor` — three semantic sliders, a live sentence preview, and a warning when a selected tone conflicts with a high-stress context. This makes the deck's “famous cursor” operational rather than decorative.

### 10. Build a content system

Explain when a voice chart, glossary, component guidelines, reusable microcopy, and content assistant become useful. Include a compact example record: preferred term, avoided synonym, component, context, rationale, owner, and review date (slides 88–94).

**Article-only section:** a worked content-system entry is more valuable than another quiz.

### 11. Accessibility and inclusion are writing work

Cover the UX-law table, descriptive links, visible labels, short/scannable structure, meaningful headings, no color-only meaning, inclusive terminology, relationship-neutral questions, and non-presumptive defaults (slides 96–105). Explain accessibility and inclusion separately, then show where they overlap.

**Add:** `AccessibilityPass` — identify problems in a small form and rewrite the link, label, assumption, and default value. The component already exists in `ux-writing-lab.tsx` but is not rendered.

### 12. Use AI without giving away judgment

Replace the removed “Using AI wisely” summary card with a real chapter. Teach a prompt brief with: role, user/context, component, desired outcome, constraints, source material, output format, and evaluation criteria. Then show four safe jobs: generate alternatives, challenge tone, simplify/flag complexity, and cluster research with citations back to the source. Close with verification, confidentiality, and governance (slides 107–120).

**Add:** `PromptWorkbench` — toggle context fields on/off and compare generic versus grounded output. The answer should emphasize that a longer prompt is not inherently better; relevant context and an evaluation rubric are.

### 13. Capstone: audit and rewrite one flow

End with a three-screen beginner flow that combines the course instead of another takeaway card: define the user goal, find the copy problems, rewrite key components, select a tone, run the accessibility pass, and state what to test (slide 122).

**Add or make optional:** `FinalAudit` — reuse the smaller labs' feedback patterns, show progress across six checks, and finish with a concise audit summary the learner can copy.

## Reusable exercise design rules

- Introduce the principle before asking the learner to perform it.
- Use one decision per screen and reveal feedback next to that decision.
- Explain why an answer works in terms of user goal, context, and next action; avoid a bare “Correct.”
- Preserve selected state after feedback so the learner can compare options.
- Let retries teach a different rule instead of resetting without explanation.
- Use the same card shell, type scale, internal spacing, selected state, and feedback motion across every lab.
- Do not reserve hidden vertical space for feedback; insert it only after the answer appears.
- Avoid color-only correctness. Pair color with a label and icon.
- Keep the capstone's results on the page so the learner can review the whole decision trail.
